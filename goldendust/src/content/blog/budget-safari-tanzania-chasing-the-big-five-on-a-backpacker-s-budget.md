We're based in Arusha, and every week we meet backpackers who've just stepped off the bus and been surrounded by people selling [safari packages](/product-page/tarangire-serengeti-ngorongoro-safari-5d4n-budget). Everyone has the "best price, best tour". Most of them are not lying about the tour. Many of them are lying about the price.

So here's how to do a budget safari in Tanzania properly: what it really costs, where you can save, where you can't, and what's worth knowing before you hand over your money.

## First, the bad news

Safaris in Tanzania aren't cheap, however you do them. The biggest reason is park fees. The Serengeti is around $80 per person per day, camping inside the park adds roughly $30 a night, and that's before the vehicle, the guide, fuel and food. These fees are set by the government. No operator can discount them, however hard you bargain.

That's useful to know, because it tells you where the real savings are: everywhere *except* the fees.

## Haggling in Arusha

Opening quotes in town can be wild. We regularly hear of 3-day Serengeti and Ngorongoro camping trips being offered at around $2,000 to solo travelers, and of quotes dropping from $1,500 to $600 after some firm back-and-forth. Bargaining is normal here, for safaris, taxis, souvenirs and even water at the market, and walking away is part of it.

But a word of warning from people on the other side of the table: if a price drops a long way, something has to give. Ask what's been cut. Are park fees included? How many people will be in the vehicle? Who is the guide, and is the operator licensed? A cheap trip with a broken-down car and an unlicensed guide isn't a bargain.

Our advice: get two or three written quotes, check exactly what's included, and only then compare prices. Our [safari cost breakdown](/post/how-much-does-a-tanzania-safari-cost-complete-budget-breakdown) shows what each part should roughly cost.

## Where the real savings are

**Share the vehicle.** This is the biggest one. A vehicle and guide cost the same whether one person or four are in it. Team up with other travelers from your hostel, or ask an operator to put you in a small group. Just know the trade-off: the more people, the less say you have over where you stop and for how long.

**Camp.** Camping in the public campsites inside the parks is the cheapest way to sleep on safari, and honestly it's one of the best. There are no fences. You'll hear hyenas whooping while you cook, lions in the distance at night, and sometimes a buffalo grazing right outside your tent. Walking to the toilet block in the dark with a headlamp will get your heart going. Many people tell us those nights were the highlight of the trip.

There's no hot shower or buffet, though. Usually a cook travels with you and makes simple, filling meals, ugali and stew at camp and packed lunches under an acacia (keep an eye on the vervet monkeys).

**Travel just before or after peak season.** Late May or early June, or November, means quieter parks and lower prices. In peak months the popular sightings get crowded. We've seen two dozen vehicles around a single rhino in the Ngorongoro Crater, and that's not the wilderness anyone pays for.

**Save in town.** Hostel dorms, street food and the daladala (local minibus) cost very little, and leave more money for days in the parks.

## The day trip most backpackers skip

Everyone rushes to the Serengeti, but Arusha National Park is right on our doorstep and costs a fraction in fees. There are no lions, but you can do a walking safari with an armed ranger and see giraffes and buffalo on foot, which you can't do in most of the big parks. You can canoe on the Momella Lakes among flamingos, and walk through misty forest at the foot of Mount Meru, where black-and-white colobus monkeys watch you from the trees.

You can get there by daladala and do it as a day trip from Arusha. It's a great extra day after a big safari, or a cheap taste of the bush if your budget only stretches to one day.

## A note on village visits

Many budget trips include a stop at a Maasai boma, with the jumping dance, a look inside a house and a craft market at the end. Some of these visits are good, and some are mostly a show built around selling. If cultural contact matters to you, ask how the fee is shared with the community, or ask for a community-run visit or homestay. A good operator will know which ones are genuine.
